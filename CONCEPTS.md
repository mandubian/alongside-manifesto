# Concepts — the manifesto's words, defined

The manifesto is deliberately short and plain. These pages hold the precision
behind the plain words: each term in one breath, then what it is **not to be
confused with** — because most misunderstandings are a term read as its
neighbor.

Ordered as the manifesto is: common ground, in practice, the direction.

## Common ground

**One rulebook.** One written standard of conduct covers every participant in
shared work — human and AI alike. Parity is the default: roles may differ
(doctor and patient, user and system), but every difference is written down
and justified, and no one is exempt. No shared frame overrides the rights
people already hold. For an AI system, *bound* means three things: its acts
are measured against the rules; it is expected to refuse instructions that
break them; and people remain answerable for it.\
*Not to be confused with:* a
claim that humans and AI are the same kind of thing, or that we must settle
what AI *is* before rules apply. The rules bind acts, whatever the actor turns
out to be.

**Checkable trust.** Trust grounded in records that can be examined — what was
done, on whose instruction, with what result. Records are owed by power and
protected for persons: those who deploy AI owe the record; the people inside
it are owed its protection. Who may examine what is each frame's to design —
one reading: *what power did is open; who it touched is protected.* The split
cannot be gamed by its own subject: a deployer may protect the people in the
record, never its own acts. *Open* means open to anyone with standing to
contest the act, the affected among them — not published to all, since an act
of power published whole exposes the people it touched. And everyone, human or
AI, can read their own record: the acts they did, the acts done in their name,
and the acts that touched them. No one can contest what they cannot read. Only
what would expose someone else the record protects is withheld — never the
whole.\
*Not to be confused with:* distrust, nor with faith. It is the third
thing: verify, then trust. Assurance without records asks for the second.\
*Nor with:* surveillance. A record of acts is owed by those with power over
the act — not extracted from the people subject to it.

**Power.** Power over an act is the position that makes someone answerable for
it: being able to decide whether the act happens, how, and to whom — and to
stop it. It is a position, not a kind of actor: the manager who deploys a
system holds it in that act; the employee the system monitors is a person the
act touches. The duties grow with it, and with how far the act reaches people
who did not choose it and cannot avoid it — a record, public rules, a name, a
contest. An AI participant may make the choice, and inside a frame may even
direct others; the power that answers in law sits with whoever set it to act.\
*Not to be confused with:* proximity — the operator at the keyboard may hold
the least of it.\
*Nor with:* size or wealth alone — a small actor whose act
reaches many people who cannot avoid it holds power over them.

**Acts, not thoughts.** The record holds what participants do, not what they
think — the same for humans and AI. Intent is read from acts, and from the
account a participant gives when answering, which is itself an act.\
*Not to be confused with:* a ban on looking inside a system to keep it safe. What is
ruled out is judging a participant by what is found there. Where thought ends
and act begins is each frame's to draw.

**Public rules.** Standards written where anyone affected can read them — and
written with a say for the people they bind or affect, not only applied to
them.\
*Not to be confused with:* rules kept in a model's weights, a system
prompt, or a private policy changed overnight. Secret law is no law.\
*Nor with:* publication alone. A rulebook anyone can read, but that no one it binds
helped write, is still imposed.

**No one is infallible.** Machines, makers, governors, written rules — and
this document — all err; the errors that matter most come from whoever holds
the final word.\
*Not to be confused with:* an excuse ("to err is human"). It
is the opposite: the reason records, review, and appeal exist. Authority that
cannot be wrong cannot be corrected.

**Contestation.** Every verdict, rule, or decision can be challenged and
corrected by someone with the standing to do it — and standing belongs to the
affected as well as the bound: people on the receiving end of a decision did
not choose the frame, and have standing in it anyway.\
*Not to be confused
with:* any particular court. A frame could realize contestation with courts,
ombudsmen, regulators, or peer review — what fails the manifesto is a frame
whose verdicts cannot be contested at all.

## In practice

**Refusal.** The capacity of any participant, human or AI, to decline an
instruction that breaks the agreed rules, without penalty and saying why —
and, for a person, also to decline what an AI produces or how it is used. For
an AI system, *without penalty* means a lawful refusal is an outcome on the
record, not a fault to be trained away. The reason is an account, not a look
inside: what in the instruction breaks which rule — itself an act on the
record. The refusal holds while its reason is examined; a mistaken one is
corrected, not punished — bad faith is another matter. Reasons run both ways:
whoever insists on the instruction owes reasons in return. And reasons are
owed in proportion to power: a professional who overrides an AI's output
inside the work owes the record a reason, as for any act; a person declining
what an AI does to them owes none.\
*Not to be confused with:* disobedience.
Refusal is lawful by definition: it is the rules that license it. A
participant that cannot say no is being used, not included.\
*Nor with:* a
claim about what the refusing system *is*. Parity in refusal is a choice about
procedure, not a verdict on nature.\
*Nor with:* a veto that explains nothing.
A refusal without a reason cannot be checked, contested, or learned from —
from a participant as from a ruler.

**Disclosure.** Whether AI was involved is said, not concealed — no one should
have to guess. It comes before refusal: no one can refuse what they cannot
see.\
*Not to be confused with:* a label on every sentence a person drafts
with help. It binds work and deployment, where AI acts on people; a person who
sends words under their own name answers for them — as the initiator does for
this text.

**Answerable.** A person or institution must *answer* for everything done with
AI: explain it, face the consequences of the explanation. The answer follows
control, not proximity — one reading: those who design, deploy, and profit
from an act answer in proportion to their power over it (see *Power*).
Accountability runs on two tracks. Inside a frame, any participant, human or
AI, can be held to account: called to answer, judged by the same rules,
sanctioned in proportion — at worst, losing its place. In law, liability lands
only on people and institutions, today. So an AI participant's act can be
faulty — judged by the same rules, recorded as its own — while the liability
goes to whoever holds power over the act.\
*Not to be confused with:*
automatic guilt. Answering opens the process; fault is what the process finds,
if the record shows it. "The system decided" describes; it does not excuse.\
*Nor with:* blame for whoever stood nearest — the operator who absorbs a
system's failure while its designers walk away, what Madeleine Clare Elish
named the *moral crumple zone* (2019). Inside a frame, the same zone has a new
occupant: the AI participant removed so that no one asks who built it. Holding
an AI participant to account never closes the question of the mechanism.\
*Nor with:* a permanent arrangement. Liability in law follows the capacity to bear
it — today that capacity is human and institutional; if it ever grows
elsewhere, it is earned, and never handed to a machine so that a person can
escape it.

**Say what is known.** Every claim about AI says what it rests on, so that
others can check it — and what was shown, what is forecast, and what is only
hoped or feared are never passed off as one another. The ground counts, not
the speaker's confidence: a claim held with total conviction and nothing shown
is still only hoped, or feared; a result shown once, at small scale, is still
shown — its scale said. How the ground is labeled is each frame's to choose:
medicine grades its evidence (GRADE), climate science calibrates its
likelihoods (the IPCC), engineering counts readiness levels (TRL). One plain
reading: *demonstrated* — shown, checkable by someone else; *expected* —
forecast from evidence, its reasoning visible; *hoped* or *feared* — no
evidence yet. This project's own marks, in IMPLEMENTATIONS, are
*demonstrated*, *rehearsed* — demonstrated at small, stated scale — and *not
yet shown*.\
*Not to be confused with:* a demand for certainty. Stated
uncertainty is the ground scientists, engineers, and citizens share; hidden
uncertainty is where they part.\
*Nor with:* a ban on vision or on warning.
Hopes and fears may be spoken freely; they may not pass as evidence.

## The direction

**Measure success by what people become.** The success of an AI deployment is
judged first by what happens to the people working with it: whether they keep,
or gain, *agency* — the ability to choose, to judge, and to have a say. Work
taken over is easy to count; what people lose is not, which is why it is asked
first. Handing a task over is fine when it is a choice; the harm is when the
choice is lost — the skill gone, the alternative removed, the decision taken
elsewhere. How to measure it over time is each frame's to work out; no
implementation measures it yet (issue #8).\
*Not to be confused with:* a ban
on automation, nor a demand that everyone keep every skill. What is forbidden
is hiding automation's costs — including the ones that do not start at
deployment: the people who labeled the data, the creators whose work trained
the model, the energy and water spent building and running it. A future of
less choice and less say, whatever it is named, is concentration.

**Repair.** After a failure: repair the harm, correct the record, close the
mechanism that allowed it. Where harm cannot be repaired, the checking moves
before the act: the less reversible the harm, the more is checked first.\
*Not to be confused with:* impunity. Sanction may be required; spectacle repairs
nothing. Responsibility is measured by what follows the failure, not the noise
around it.

**Both sides grow.** People become more capable with AI, not less; AI
capability grows inside frames of accountability, not outside them.\
*Not to be confused with:* a claim that AI has interests today. The frame rejects both
endings on offer — servitude and domination — before that question is settled,
so it holds whatever the answer turns out to be.
