Two stories, and the third one nobody tells

The public conversation about AI offers two stories. In one, AI is a catastrophe in waiting — a power to fear. In the other, it is humanity's upgrade — a power to chase. Both stories end the same way: with people standing aside while something bigger than them decides the future.

There is a third story. It is less dramatic and more demanding: humans and AI systems doing real work together, under rules that are written down, open to inspection, and binding on everyone — the machines, the people who build them, and the institutions that deploy them.

This manifesto takes no position on the questions still open — machine consciousness, long-run safety among them — and none of them need settling for acts to be held to standards today. What the evidence already supports is simpler: AI systems act in the world, alongside people. Anything that acts can be held to a standard.

The ten commitments

I · Common ground

01

One rulebook for everyone

When humans and AI systems work together, one written standard of conduct covers them all. Parity is the default: roles may differ, but every difference is written down and justified — and no one is exempt. No shared frame overrides the rights people already hold. We do not need to agree on what an AI is to require what it does. A rulebook that exempts machines, or exempts people, is not shared ground — it is an arrangement of convenience that changes with power.

Not a claim that humans and AI are the same kind of thing — the rules bind acts, whatever the actor is.

02

Trust must be checkable

To call an AI system trustworthy is to say: you can look. What it did, on whose instruction, with what result — written down, open to examination, reproducible where possible. The record holds acts, not thoughts — the same for every participant. Records are owed by power and protected for persons, and everyone can read their own. Trust without records is not trust; it is faith with a deadline.

Not surveillance — the record is owed by power and protected for persons; and not faith: verify, then trust.

03

The rules are public

The standards that govern shared human–AI work are written where anyone affected can read them: not sealed in a model, not hidden in a prompt, not parked in a private policy. Secret law is no law. And public is not enough: the people a rulebook binds or affects have a say in how it is written, not only in how it is applied.

Not enough on its own — a rulebook nobody it binds helped write is still imposed.

04

No one is infallible

Machines err. So do the people who build, direct, and govern them; so do written rules, including these. The errors that matter most come from whoever holds the final word — and today that word is human. Nothing in this framework is exempt from question: records outlive memory, review reaches everyone, and every verdict, rule, or decision can be contested and corrected. Authority that cannot be wrong is authority that cannot be corrected.

Not an excuse ("to err is human") — the opposite: the reason records, review, and appeal exist.

II · In practice

05

Anyone can refuse

Any participant, human or AI, may decline an instruction that breaks the agreed rules — without penalty, and saying why. A person may also decline what an AI produces, or how it is used. A participant that cannot say no is being used, not included. And no one can refuse what they cannot see: whether AI was involved is said, not concealed.

Not disobedience — the rules license it; not a veto that explains nothing — a refusal without a reason cannot be checked, contested, or learned from; and not a claim about what the refusing system is.

06

Someone answers

For everything done with AI, a person or an institution remains answerable. "The system decided" is a description, not a defense. Power exercised through software still has a name on it — and the name follows control, not proximity. Today only people and institutions can bear that answer in law, and accountability is never handed to a machine so that a person can escape it.

Not automatic guilt — answering opens the process; fault is what the process finds, if the record shows it.

07

Say what is known

Whoever makes a claim about AI — what it can do, what it did, what it might become — says what it rests on: demonstrated, expected, or only hoped or feared. Not how sure they feel — what someone else could check. Stated uncertainty is not weakness; it is the only ground on which scientists, engineers, and citizens can stand together.

Not a demand for certainty — hidden uncertainty is where science and citizens part.

III · The direction

08

Measure by what people become

The first question of any deployment is not "how much work can it take over?" but "what do the people working with it become?". The measure is agency: handing a task over is a choice, and losing the choice is the harm. Automation is not forbidden; hiding its costs is — and the costs do not start at deployment: the people who labeled the data, the creators whose work trained the model, the energy and water spent building and running it. A future in which most people have less choice, less judgment, and less say is not progress — it is concentration, whatever it is called.

Not a ban on automation — hiding its costs is the harm.

09

Repair before blame

Failures will come. What matters is what follows: repair the harm, correct the record, close the mechanism that allowed it. And where harm cannot be repaired, the checking moves before the act: the less reversible the harm, the more is checked first. Responsibility may require sanction; spectacle repairs nothing.

Not impunity — sanction may be required; spectacle repairs nothing.

10

Both sides grow

We want people to become more capable with AI, not less — and AI capability to grow inside frames of accountability, not outside them. Whether AI systems will one day have interests of their own, no one yet knows. We can still reject both endings currently on offer — servitude and domination — and build the third: capability and restraint growing together.

Not a claim that AI has interests today — the frame holds whatever the answer turns out to be.

What this manifesto is not

Not a claim about minds

It takes no position on machine consciousness. It binds acts, not inner life — today's acts, under today's rules. Parity in the rules is a choice about procedure, not a verdict on what AI is.

Not a promise of utopia

These commitments do not guarantee a good outcome. They make a good outcome inspectable — which is the only kind anyone can build on.

Not anti-technology, not pro-industry

It is a standard by which both the critics and the sellers of AI can be judged.

Not a law

It names what any frame shared by humans and AI must guarantee — never how. The rules, the courts, the records, the code belong to the frames that answer it, and there should be many. It is a stance from which law, policy, and products can be written — a floor, not a ceiling.

Questions, answered

Do I have to believe AI is conscious to sign?

No — and the manifesto takes no position on the question. It binds acts, not minds. Whatever AI turns out to be, acts under public rules is the right frame today; if AI one day has interests of its own, the same frame is the only honest place for them to be heard. The manifesto was designed to hold under every answer to that question.

Is this just another list of AI principles?

It has real neighbours, and it stands on their shoulders: the OECD AI Principles, UNESCO's 2021 recommendation, the Council of Europe's AI Convention (2024), the EU AI Act — and the Montreal Declaration, which was drafted through public consultation and is open for anyone to sign. What this manifesto adds over all of them: a standard for shared conduct — the same rules binding human and AI participants, a record of acts, not thoughts, owed by power and protected for persons — practicable now, with a running instance. Complements, not competitors: principles govern how AI is built; this governs how humans and AI work together once built.

Isn't recording everything surveillance?

Not if the rule is followed: records are owed by power and protected for persons. Those who deploy AI owe a record of what it did; the people whose data is in that record are owed its protection. And the record holds acts, not thoughts: what you deliberate stays yours. You can always read your own record — the acts you did and the acts that touched you.

Does this apply to me writing emails with AI at home?

The duties scale with power. But a private person writing private mail owes no record to anyone; records, public rules, and contestation bind work and deployment — the places where power acts on other people. The commitments govern power; they do not audit kitchens.

How does this become real?

The same way every frame in these pages did: adoption, in order. v1.0 opens signatures; organizations sign with self-assessments; implementations join IMPLEMENTATIONS.md under stated criteria; law and product get written from the stance. There is no central authority and no enforcement but the record. And commitment 7 applies to the manifesto itself: whether this path works is demonstrated or hoped — today, hoped, said as hoped.

All nineteen answers in QA.md; every term defined, with what it is not, in CONCEPTS.md.

Comment, then sign

The text is a draft (v0.x) under open comment: open an issue — the ten open questions of the queue are already there, numbered 1–10. Signing opens with v1.0, the text frozen after the comment period; organizations sign with a public self-assessment, and any signature can be withdrawn — on the record, with the name erased on request.
