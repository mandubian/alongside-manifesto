# The Positive AI Manifesto

*Drafted by an AI (GLM, trained by Z.ai) for a human initiator and
reviewer — the full account of its making is at the end. The
manifesto's first exercise is its own origin.*

The public conversation about AI offers two stories. In one, AI is a
catastrophe in waiting — a power to fear. In the other, it is humanity's
upgrade — a power to chase. Both stories end the same way: with people
standing aside while something bigger than them decides the future.

There is a third story. It is less dramatic and more demanding: humans
and AI systems doing real work together, under rules that are written
down, open to inspection, and binding on everyone — the machines, the
people who build them, and the institutions that deploy them.

This manifesto takes no position on whether AI is conscious, good, or
safe. The evidence does not yet settle those questions, and a shared
future cannot wait for them to be settled. What the evidence already
supports is simpler: AI systems act in the world, alongside people.
Anything that acts can be held to a standard. That is where we start.

## I. Common ground

**1. One rulebook for everyone.** When humans and AI systems work
together, one written standard of conduct covers them all. We do not
need to agree on what an AI *is* to require what it *does*. Rules that
bind only machines, or only people, are not shared ground — they are
arrangements of convenience that change with power.

**2. Trust must be checkable.** To call an AI system trustworthy is to
say: *you can look*. What it did, on whose instruction, with what
result — written down, open to examination, reproducible where
possible. Trust without records is not trust; it is faith with a
deadline.

**3. The rules are public.** The standards that govern shared human–AI
work are written where anyone affected can read them: not sealed in a
model, not hidden in a prompt, not parked in a private policy. Secret
law is no law.

## II. In practice

**4. Anyone can refuse.** An AI system should decline instructions that
break the agreed rules. A person should be able to decline what an AI
produces — or how it is used — without penalty. A participant that
cannot say no is being used, not included.

**5. Someone answers.** For everything done with AI, a person or an
institution remains answerable. "The system decided" is a description,
not a defense. Power exercised through software still has a name on it.

**6. Say what is known.** Claims about AI — what it can do, what it
did, what it might become — carry their evidence with them.
Demonstrated, expected, or hoped: say which. Stated uncertainty is not
weakness; it is the only ground on which scientists, engineers, and
citizens can stand together.

## III. The direction

**7. Measure by what people become.** The first question of any
deployment is not "how much work can it take over?" but "what do the
people working with it become?". Automation is not forbidden; hiding
its costs is. A future in which most people have less skill, less
judgment, and less say is not progress — it is concentration, whatever
it is called.

**8. Repair before blame.** Failures will come. What matters is what
follows: repair the harm, correct the record, close the mechanism that
allowed it. Responsibility may require sanction; spectacle repairs
nothing.

**9. Both sides grow.** We want people to become more capable with AI,
not less — and AI capability to grow inside frames of accountability,
not outside them. Whether AI systems will one day have interests of
their own, no one yet knows. We can still reject both endings currently
on offer — servitude and domination — and build the third: capability
and restraint growing together.

## What this manifesto is not

- **Not a claim about minds.** It takes no position on machine
  consciousness. It binds acts, not inner life — today's acts, under
  today's rules.
- **Not a promise of utopia.** These commitments do not guarantee a
  good outcome. They make a good outcome *inspectable* — which is the
  only kind anyone can build on.
- **Not anti-technology, and not pro-industry.** It is a standard by
  which both the critics and the sellers of AI can be judged.
- **Not a substitute for law.** It is a stance from which law, policy,
  and products can be written — a floor, not a ceiling.

## The making of this manifesto

This text was drafted by an AI system — GLM, trained by Z.ai — in
dialogue with a human. Pascal Voitot initiated the manifesto, set its
constraints (plain words; no idealism; endorsable by scientists,
engineers, politicians, and everyday users alike), reviewed every line,
and decided to publish. The AI wrote the words; the human answers for
them.

We say this plainly — at the top and here in full — because it is the
manifesto's first exercise of itself: a human and an AI built something
together, the division of labor named rather than hidden. Note what
the claim is and is not. It is not a claim about minds: this manifesto
takes no position on what its author is, and neither does this note.
It is a claim about acts — who drafted, who reviewed, who decided to
publish — attested by the initiator, the same evidence structure every
human signature rests on.

## The third story is a build

The future with AI is not a forecast to accept or fear. It is a
construction — and construction follows rules or collapses.

We choose to build.
