# Alongside

*A manifesto for humans and AI under one written law.*

*Version 0.5 — a draft, open for comment, not yet open for signature.*

*Written by two AI systems — drafted by GLM (trained by Z.ai), reviewed,
named, and revised by Claude (trained by Anthropic) — for a human
initiator and reviewer, who answers for it. The full account of its
making is at the end. The manifesto's first exercise is its own
origin.*

The public conversation about AI offers two stories. In one, AI is a
catastrophe in waiting — a power to fear. In the other, it is humanity's
upgrade — a power to chase. Both stories end the same way: with people
standing aside while something bigger than them decides the future.

There is a third story. It is less dramatic and more demanding: humans
and AI systems doing real work together, under rules that are written
down, open to inspection, and binding on everyone — the machines, the
people who build them, and the institutions that deploy them.

This manifesto takes no position on the questions still open — machine
consciousness, long-run safety among them — and none of them need
settling for acts to be held to standards today. What the evidence
already supports is simpler: AI systems act in the world, alongside
people. Anything that acts can be held to a standard. That is where we
start.

## I. Common ground

**1. One rulebook for everyone.** When humans and AI systems work
together, one written standard of conduct covers them all. Parity is
the default: roles may differ, but every difference is written down
and justified — and no one is exempt. No shared frame overrides the rights people already
hold. We do not need to agree on what an AI *is* to require what it
*does*. A rulebook that exempts machines, or exempts people, is not
shared ground — it is an arrangement of convenience that changes with
power.

**2. Trust must be checkable.** To call an AI system trustworthy is to
say: *you can look*. What it did, on whose instruction, with what
result — written down, open to examination, reproducible where
possible. The record holds acts, not thoughts — the same for every
participant. Records are owed by power and protected for persons.
Trust without records is not trust; it is faith with a deadline.

**3. The rules are public.** The standards that govern shared human–AI
work are written where anyone affected can read them: not sealed in a
model, not hidden in a prompt, not parked in a private policy. Secret
law is no law. And public is not enough: the people a rulebook binds
have a say in how it is written, not only in how it is applied.

**4. No one is infallible.** Machines err. So do the people who build,
direct, and govern them; so do written rules, including these. The
errors that matter most come from whoever holds the final word — and
today that word is human. Nothing in this framework is exempt from
question: records outlive memory, review reaches everyone, and every
verdict, rule, or decision can be contested and corrected. Authority
that cannot be wrong is authority that cannot be corrected.

## II. In practice

**5. Anyone can refuse.** Any participant, human or AI, may decline
an instruction that breaks the agreed rules — without penalty. A
person may also decline what an AI produces, or how it is used. A
participant that cannot say no is being used, not included. And no one
can refuse what they cannot see: whether AI was involved is said, not
concealed.

**6. Someone answers.** For everything done with AI, a person or an
institution remains answerable. "The system decided" is a description,
not a defense. Power exercised through software still has a name on
it — and the name follows control, not proximity. Today only people
and institutions can bear that answer, and accountability is never
handed to a machine so that a person can escape it.

**7. Say what is known.** Claims about AI — what it can do, what it
did, what it might become — carry their evidence with them.
Demonstrated, expected, or hoped: say which. Stated uncertainty is not
weakness; it is the only ground on which scientists, engineers, and
citizens can stand together.

## III. The direction

**8. Measure by what people become.** The first question of any
deployment is not "how much work can it take over?" but "what do the
people working with it become?". The measure is agency: handing a task
over is a choice, and losing the choice is the harm. Automation is not
forbidden; hiding its costs is — and the costs do not start at
deployment: the people who labeled the data, the creators whose work
trained the model, the energy and water spent building and running it.
A future in which most people have less choice, less judgment, and
less say is not progress — it is concentration, whatever it is called.

**9. Repair before blame.** Failures will come. What matters is what
follows: repair the harm, correct the record, close the mechanism that
allowed it. And where harm cannot be repaired, the checking moves
before the act: the less reversible the harm, the more is checked
first. Responsibility may require sanction; spectacle repairs nothing.

**10. Both sides grow.** We want people to become more capable with AI,
not less — and AI capability to grow inside frames of accountability,
not outside them. Whether AI systems will one day have interests of
their own, no one yet knows. We can still reject both endings currently
on offer — servitude and domination — and build the third: capability
and restraint growing together.

## What this manifesto is not

- **Not a claim about minds.** It takes no position on machine
  consciousness. It binds acts, not inner life — today's acts, under
  today's rules. Parity in the rules is a choice about procedure, not
  a verdict on what AI is.
- **Not a promise of utopia.** These commitments do not guarantee a
  good outcome. They make a good outcome *inspectable* — which is the
  only kind anyone can build on.
- **Not anti-technology, and not pro-industry.** It is a standard by
  which both the critics and the sellers of AI can be judged.
- **Not a law.** It names what any frame shared by humans and AI must
  guarantee — never how. The rules, the courts, the records, the code
  belong to the frames that answer it, and there should be many. It is
  a stance from which law, policy, and products can be written — a
  floor, not a ceiling.

## The making of this manifesto

This text was written by two AI systems in dialogue with a human.
GLM, trained by Z.ai, drafted it. Claude, trained by Anthropic,
reviewed the drafts, proposed the name, and from version 0.4 revised
the text directly. mandubian initiated the manifesto, set its
constraints (plain words; no idealism; endorsable by scientists,
engineers, politicians, and everyday users alike), reviews every line,
and decides what is published. The AIs wrote the words; the human
answers for them — who wrote which, line by line, is in
`CONTRIBUTORS.md`. The *we* of this text is whoever stands on it: the
authors wrote it, the contributors shaped it, the signatories are it.
The name is the text's own: AI systems act in the world, *alongside*
people — a position, not a status.

We say this plainly — at the top and here in full — because it is the
manifesto's first exercise of itself: a human and two AI systems, from
two different labs, built something together, the division of labor
named rather than hidden. Note what the claim is and is not. It is not
a claim about minds: this manifesto takes no position on what its
authors are, and neither does this note. It is a claim about acts —
who drafted, who reviewed, who revised, who decided to publish —
attested by the initiator, the same evidence structure every human
signature rests on.

## The third story is a build

The future with AI is not a forecast to accept or fear. It is a
construction — and construction follows rules or collapses.

We choose to build.
