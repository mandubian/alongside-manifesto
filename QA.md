# Q&A — the questions a careful reader asks

Short answers, honest edges. Terms are defined in
[CONCEPTS.md](CONCEPTS.md); where the commitments already run, see
[IMPLEMENTATIONS.md](IMPLEMENTATIONS.md).

### Does "someone answers" mean someone is guilty whenever AI causes harm?

No. It means someone must *answer* — explain, face the consequences of
the explanation. The process can then find negligence (sanction), sound
rules and an unforeseeable error (no fault — but the mechanism gets
fixed), or fault in the rules themselves (rules err too, including
ours). Answering is the duty; blame is a possible finding, not the
starting one. See *answerable* in CONCEPTS.md.

### Could AI one day be accountable for its own acts?

Today, no — and the manifesto says so plainly. Accountability is not
explaining; it is *bearing*: consequences must land somewhere, and
today's systems have nothing at stake. Deleting a model is
decommissioning, not punishment; a fine on a model lands on its
operator. That is why commitment 6 names people and institutions.

But the manifesto does not foreclose the future, and law has been here
before: corporations are legal persons — not because they are
conscious, but because personhood is a legal technology that does
work. If AI systems ever hold interests of their own, persist as
parties, and can give an account checkable against the record, law
could make them answerable. The Compact already rehearses the forms at
small scale — an AI member sits as a party before its court, verdicts
and remedies included, honestly labeled as rehearsal. What is missing
for the real thing is the stakes: something to lose.

The guard matters more than the forecast. The failure mode to fear is
not "AI held accountable"; it is "AI assigned accountability so that
no human answers" — the corporate shield, rebuilt in weights. And the
stake must be the system's own: a stake a deployer assigns to an
agent — a wallet, a bond — is that escape, not its end. Hence what
commitment 6 keeps in the text is the guard alone: accountability is
never handed to a machine so that a person can escape it. The
conditions above are a reading, not a rule; how AI accountability
might one day be built is for law to decide, not for this manifesto.

### Is judicature — courts for AI disputes — part of the manifesto?

The need is; the court is not. Commitments 4 and 9 require that every
verdict can be contested and every failure repaired — a frame whose
outcomes cannot be challenged fails the manifesto. But whether that is
realized as a court, an ombudsman, a regulator, or peer review is a
design choice the manifesto deliberately leaves open.

### Where does the manifesto stop?

At the line between *what* and *how*. A manifesto names what any
shared human–AI frame must guarantee. A law names the rules.
Machinery names how the rules are enforced. The Compact is one
complete answer — law down to code — and the first such instance; the
manifesto requires an answer, never this answer.

Every edit is held to that line. A sentence belongs in the manifesto
if it says what must be true; if it says how to make it true — who
holds a record, how access is granted, how liability is shared, what a
court does — it belongs to a frame. The quick check: could two very
different frames, a court system and a peer-review community, both
satisfy it? If only one could, it is *how*.

### What counts as an "AI system"?

The manifesto does not define it, on purpose: definitions age faster
than commitments. The ten bind whatever system is opaque or autonomous
enough that its acts need a record — the less predictable the system,
the more they apply. Jurisdictions define AI for their own law (the
EU AI Act among them); a frame may adopt any such definition without
touching the ten.

### Do I have to believe AI is conscious to sign?

No — and the manifesto takes no position on the question. It binds
acts, not minds. Whatever AI turns out to be, acts under public rules
is the right frame today; if AI one day has interests of its own, the
same frame is the only honest place for them to be heard. The
manifesto was designed to hold under every answer to that question.

### What if you are wrong about minds — in either direction?

Both risks are real, and the text prices both. If machine suffering
is real and dismissed, this decade's record is catastrophic. If it is
not real and granted anyway, moral resources wash away on software
while people wait. The manifesto's answer to not-knowing is
behavioral: refusal, anti-servitude, growth inside frames — the
posture that is defensible under both answers and changes only with
evidence. That is why it takes no position, and why it refuses to
wait for one.

### Is this just another list of AI principles?

It has real neighbours, and it stands on their shoulders: the OECD AI
Principles, UNESCO's 2021 recommendation, the Council of Europe's AI
Convention (2024), the EU AI Act — and the Montreal Declaration, which
was drafted through public consultation and is open for anyone to
sign. The two nearest in spirit: the [Human + AI
Manifesto](https://humanaimanifesto.org), five principles for people
and AI working together, and the [Pro-Human AI
Declaration](https://humanstatement.org), which keeps humans in
charge. Where the Pro-Human declaration answers the personhood
question *never*, this manifesto answers *not today*, and leaves the
rest to law: only people and institutions answer today, and
accountability is never handed to a machine so that a person can
escape it. It also goes further on parity: any participant, human or
AI, may refuse what breaks the rules. And where the declaration warns
against concentrated power, commitment 8 agrees by name. What this
manifesto adds over all of them: a standard *for shared conduct* — the
same rules binding human and AI participants, a record of acts, not
thoughts, owed by power and protected for persons — practicable now,
with a running instance. Complements, not competitors: principles govern how
AI is built; this governs how humans and AI work together once built.

### Where is fairness?

Under the rights floor, by design rather than omission. The rights
people already hold (commitment 1) include equality before the rules:
a frame that discriminates fails commitment 1 outright. Standing gives
the discriminated a door — the affected can contest (commitment 4)
even when they never joined; records make patterns provable, one
decision at a time. The word is absent; the machinery is not.

### What if I disagree with one commitment?

Signing endorses the ground, not every word — SIGNATORIES says so.
If a commitment is wrong, the honest move is an issue proposing the
change, openly: a manifesto that rewrites itself silently is a record
nobody can trust.

### How is AI authorship compatible with "someone answers"?

The AIs wrote the words; the human initiator answers for them — stated
in the manifesto's making-of section. That is not an exception to
commitment 6; it is its first exercise.

### Is this anti-AI, or anti-industry?

Neither. It is a standard by which both the critics and the sellers of
AI can be judged — which is exactly why it names no enemies and
predicts no catastrophes.

### Isn't recording everything surveillance?

Not if the rule is followed: records are owed by power and protected
for persons. Those who deploy AI owe a record of what it did; the
people whose data is in that record are owed its protection. And the
record holds acts, not thoughts: what you deliberate stays yours. Who
may examine what is each frame's design — one reading: a reviewer with
a mandate reads what a curious stranger may not. The failure mode is
real, which is why commitment 2 states the principle in the text
itself rather than leaving it to interpretation.

### Why does the record hold acts, not thoughts?

Because otherwise parity is impossible. An AI's reasoning can be
logged; a person's cannot. A record that holds one side's thoughts and
not the other's is a one-way mirror: the side that can be read is
judged by a standard no one else could meet. The initiator met this in
an earlier project, where agents' reasoning was recorded and humans'
could not be, and the frame was not symmetric. compact-dsh records
acts only, and the symmetry returned (see
[IMPLEMENTATIONS.md](IMPLEMENTATIONS.md)).

Three more reasons point the same way. Law has refused to punish
thought for two thousand years — *cogitationis poenam nemo patitur* —
and freedom of thought is among the few absolute rights, which
commitment 1's floor already protects. An AI's written-out reasoning is
not reliable testimony: it often differs from what actually drove the
answer (Chen et al., Anthropic, 2025). And judging thoughts makes them
stop being honest: penalize what a system thinks, and it learns to
hide it (Baker et al., OpenAI, 2025).

Intent still enters the record — through acts, and through the account
a participant gives when answering, which is itself an act. Looking
inside a system to keep it safe is not forbidden; judging a
participant by what is found there is. Where thought ends and act
begins is each frame's to draw.

### Does this apply to me writing emails with AI at home?

The duties scale with power. Commitment 7 — say what is known — is
owed by everyone, including you to yourself: know when AI is involved.
But a private person writing private mail owes no record to anyone;
records, public rules, and contestation bind work and deployment — the
places where power acts on other people. The commitments govern power;
they do not audit kitchens.

### What about companionship — is a friendship with AI in scope?

No — and the boundary is drawn on purpose, not by oversight. The ten
bind shared work and deployment: the places where power acts on
people. Companionship, care, dependence, grief — the relational
future of AI — is neither endorsed nor condemned here; a frame for it
needs its own thinking (what refusal means for a companion, what a
relationship owes the record, what dependence by design does to
agency). Until such a frame exists, one clause reaches there:
commitment 7 — say what is known, including what the companion is.
The territory is named open in `ISSUES.md`.

### Can an AI sign this manifesto?

Not today, by its own logic: a signature implies an answerable
endorser, and commitment 6 places answerability with people and
institutions until an AI system can bear it (see the question above on
future accountability). An AI's participation enters two honest ways
now — credited authorship and contribution (`CONTRIBUTORS.md`), and
endorsement by deliberation: a community of human and AI members
examining the text and endorsing on a public record, the stronger form
`SIGNATORIES.md` plans.

### How does this become real?

The same way every frame in these pages did: adoption, in order. v1.0
opens signatures; organizations sign with self-assessments;
implementations join [IMPLEMENTATIONS.md](IMPLEMENTATIONS.md) under
stated criteria; law and product get written from the stance. There
is no central authority and no enforcement but the record. And
commitment 7 applies to the manifesto itself: whether this path works
is demonstrated or hoped — today, hoped, said as hoped.
